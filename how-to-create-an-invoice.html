How to create an invoice: step-by-step guide

An invoice is a request for payment, but it is also the document your client's accountant files, your own record for tax, and the thing a court would look at if a payment is disputed. Getting the basics right takes ten minutes and saves a month of chasing.

Updated 26 October 2026 · 6 minute read

If you just want the document, open the free invoice generator, fill the form and press Download PDF. What follows is what belongs in each field, and why.

1. Start with the two parties

Every invoice needs a clear statement of who is billing whom. Put your own business details at the top: legal or trading name, address, email, phone, and your tax registration number if you have one. Then a "bill to" block with the client's name and billing address.

Two details are worth the extra ten seconds. First, the client's legal entity name, not the brand — "Nimbus Technologies Private Limited" rather than "Nimbus", because that is the name on their purchase order and their ledger. Second, a contact for accounts, if you have one; invoices addressed to a person who has left the company sit unopened.

2. Number the invoice, and keep numbering

An invoice number does two jobs: it lets you and the client refer to the same document without ambiguity, and in most tax systems it is mandatory. In India, GST requires a consecutive serial number of up to 16 characters, unique within a financial year — you can run one series or several (say, one per branch), but you cannot skip or reuse numbers within a series.

A simple scheme is INV-2026-001, incrementing forever and resetting each financial year. If you have several clients or lines of business, a prefix helps: WEB-2026-014. The generator fills the number for you and only advances it when you press New invoice, but the field is editable if you already have your own series.

3. Date it, and set a due date

The invoice date is when the invoice is issued — normally the date you delivered the work. The due date is when payment is expected, and it should never be left to the client's imagination. "Net 15" or "Net 30" are the common terms; freelancers often use 7 or 15 days. If your contract says something else, use that.

One useful habit: issue the invoice the day you deliver, not at the end of the month. An invoice dated three weeks after delivery invites a three-week delay.

4. Describe the work in line items

Each line needs a description, a quantity, a rate and an amount. The description is where invoices get vague and payments get delayed. "Design work" tells the client nothing; "Brand identity design — logo, type system, colour palette" matches the quote they approved.

  • Quantity can be hours, days, units, or just 1 for a fixed-price item.
  • Rate is the unit price. Show the currency once, clearly, and use it throughout.
  • Group small items into one line rather than listing forty tiny entries; the client's accountant has to key them in.
  • Reference the PO or project in the description if the client gave you one. Many companies will not pay an invoice without it.

5. Handle tax and discounts properly

If you are registered for GST, the invoice needs the tax rate per line and the tax amount shown separately from the taxable value. For a supply within your own state that means CGST and SGST (half the rate each); for a supply to another state it means IGST at the full rate. The rate depends on the goods or services — 5%, 12%, 18% and 28% are the common slabs, and many services sit at 18%.

A discount should be applied before tax, not after: the taxable value is the price after discount, and GST is charged on that. If you show a discount below the tax line, your totals will not reconcile. The generator spreads a percentage or flat discount across the line items first, then calculates tax on the reduced value.

Also write the total in words below the figures. It is still a legal requirement in many jurisdictions and it is a cheap defence against someone adding a digit to a PDF.

6. Make paying you easy

The most common reason an invoice is paid late is that the client cannot pay it. Add your bank details (account name, account number, IFSC or SWIFT), or a UPI ID for Indian clients, and say what the payment reference should be — usually the invoice number.

If you accept cards or an online payment link, say so. If you charge late fees, say that too, and put it in the terms rather than introducing it after the fact.

7. Add terms, then check the total

A short terms block covers you: payment window, late-payment interest, whether the amount includes or excludes tax, and what happens on part payment. Two or three sentences is enough.

Before you send, read the numbers once with your eyes off the keyboard. The classic errors are a rate typed as 4500 instead of 45000, a quantity of 10 where you meant 1, tax applied twice, and an invoice number reused from last month.

When the client does not pay

Send a short, factual reminder three days after the due date, with the invoice attached again and the invoice number in the subject line. Follow up at two weeks, then escalate in writing. Keep the tone flat: the goal is a payment, not a point. A one-line message — "Invoice INV-2026-014 for ₹1,02,011 was due on 10 November. Could you confirm the payment date?" — works better than a paragraph.

If you invoice the same client regularly, agree the terms once in the contract and reference them rather than renegotiating each time.
